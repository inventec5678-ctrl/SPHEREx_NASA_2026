    Why SPHEREx can catch a distant planet
    
      
    SPHEREx images the whole sky every six months, from opposite sides of Earth's orbit. Anything far away
        but inside the Solar System appears to shift between those visits: that is parallax. The farther
        the object, the smaller the shift, so the size of the shift tells us the distance.
    
      
    
        
        
        
    Shift between visits six months apart: up to  arcsec
          (≈  SPHEREx pixels).
    
        
      
    
      
    Distant-body candidates in this field
    
      
      
      
      None in this field. Detections that match one distance across survey
        passes would appear here.
      
      
      
    
      

    
      
      
        
        
      1. Fetch. Every SPHEREx image covering this patch (all survey passes since May 2025) is
          requested from NASA/IPAC IRSA as a small cutout.
        2. 
        
      2. Align. Images are resampled onto one sky grid. SPHEREx's filter changes wavelength
          across the detector, so each pixel keeps its own wavelength.
        3. 
        
      3. Compare. Each image is compared with a reference built from images of the same
          detector, at the same wavelength (within 3%, pixel by pixel), taken at least 3 hours apart.
          Moving things have left by then; stars cancel.
        4. 
        
      4. Detect. Anything brighter than expected by 5 sigma becomes a candidate.
        5. 
        
      5. AI check. A small CNN, trained only on simulated movers and real artifacts,
          looks at 4-channel cutouts (image, reference, change, catalogue/ghost flags) and scores each candidate as
          a real point source or an artifact. SPHEREx's own pipeline "outlier" flag is shown separately as an
          independent hint.
        6. 
        
      6. Link. Confident detections are joined into straight-line tracks within a pass
          (asteroids), or fitted with Earth's parallax across passes (distant bodies).
        7. 
        
      7. Check. Tracks are compared with every known asteroid and comet (JPL). What is left is
          for you to inspect.
